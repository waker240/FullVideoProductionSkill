# Visual Storytelling — Tier 1 Reference

A working knowledge base of the four highest-leverage books for closing the "elegant PPT" gap in animated explainer video. Each book attacks a different failure mode of slide-like video:

- **Bruce Block, *The Visual Story*** — your sequence doesn't *build*. Teaches visual structure over time.
- **Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye*** — your cuts don't *land*. Teaches why and when to cut.
- **Williams / Thomas & Johnston (*Animator's Survival Kit* / *Illusion of Life*)** — your motion feels *placed*, not alive. Teaches the physics and timing of movement.
- **Scott McCloud, *Understanding Comics*** — your frames are *islands*. Teaches that stills-in-sequence are a language.

This document recollects the core content of each, then maps it to Remotion + static-image work. It is a reference to run scenes against, not a summary to read once.

---

## 1. Bruce Block — *The Visual Story*

### The core problem it solves

Block's premise: most people structure their *story* carefully (setups, escalation, climax, release) but leave their *visuals* to instinct or to "whatever looks nice." The result is a visual track that runs flat while the story track tries to build. The fix is to treat visual structure as a designed parameter — as deliberate as plot — and to make it rise and fall *with* the story (support) or *against* it (counterpoint), but never by accident.

He frames it as: pictures communicate to an audience through three channels — **story, sound, and visuals** — and the visual channel has its own grammar that can be controlled independently.

### The seven basic visual components

Every image — moving or still — is built from seven components. This is the vocabulary:

1. **Space** — the illusion of depth (or flatness) in the frame.
2. **Line** — the result of tonal contrast; the edges and contours that organize the frame.
3. **Shape** — the enclosed areas lines create (circle, square, triangle as the three primitives).
4. **Tone** — the range of brightness from black to white (light/dark).
5. **Color** — hue, brightness, and saturation.
6. **Movement** — motion of objects, of the camera, and within the frame.
7. **Rhythm** — the pattern of recurrence across space and time.

Block's term for these on screen: the **"visual components cast."** Just as actors appear in every shot, so do these seven — they are always communicating mood and emotion whether you direct them or not. The picture exists on a 2D **picture plane** surrounded by a **window** (the frame edge).

### The master principle: Contrast & Affinity

This is the single key that governs all seven components:

> **Contrast** (visual difference) **increases** visual intensity.
> **Affinity** (visual similarity) **decreases** visual intensity.

"Visual intensity" is felt, not measured — it's the emotional charge of an image. Bright-against-dark, warm-against-cool, fast-against-still, diagonal-against-flat: all are contrast, all crank intensity up. Similar tones, similar colors, similar movement: affinity, intensity down.

The structural move: just as a story alternates tension and release, the visuals should be controlled to alternate contrast and affinity — and the visual intensity curve should *track the story's emotional curve*. Escalating conflict → rising visual contrast. Resolution → shifting toward affinity. When the two curves contradict (calm visuals over a tense beat, chaos over a quiet one), the audience feels the mismatch even if they can't name it. That nameless mismatch is exactly what makes competent work feel "off."

### Progressions

A **progression** begins as one thing and changes into something else over the course of a sequence or whole piece. Block's argument is that a film's visual intensity should follow a *progression* — generally building toward the climax — rather than staying constant. A flat visual-intensity line is the visual signature of "PPT." The cure is a designed progression in one or more components (space opening up, contrast increasing, movement accelerating, rhythm tightening) as you approach the peak.

### The seven components in depth

**Space.** The most important and most overlooked component. Block distinguishes **deep space** (strong depth cues — overlapping planes, size differences, perspective lines, atmospheric haze, foreground/midground/background separation) from **flat space** (few depth cues, everything on one plane — the picture-postcard, the head-on graphic). Deep space is high-contrast and dynamic; flat space is high-affinity and calm. He also separates **limited space** (constrained, boxed in) from **open space**. Crucially, the *flat-vs-deep* dimension is the visual director's most powerful lever, and it's the one PPT never touches — slides are almost always relentlessly flat. A key tool: the **dominant contrast** — the one place in the frame where contrast is strongest. That's where the eye goes first. Control it and you control attention.

**Line and Shape.** Line is created by contour, by the edge of a tone, by the intersection of planes, by closure, by an axis, or by repetition implied through distance. Every line has three characteristics: **orientation** (horizontal, vertical, diagonal), **direction**, and **quality** (straight vs. curved, hard vs. soft). Horizontal and vertical lines are affinity/stability; diagonals are contrast/dynamism. The three primary shapes — **circle, square, triangle** — carry different feels; a frame dominated by one shape has affinity, a frame mixing them has contrast.

**Tone.** The brightness scale from black to white. Block stresses **tonal contrast** as a primary driver of where the eye lands (the brightest or darkest spot against its surround wins attention) and of mood — high-key (bright, low contrast) vs. low-key (dark, high contrast). Notably, line itself only exists where there is tonal contrast, so tone underlies line.

**Color.** Analyzed in three attributes: **hue**, **brightness** (value), and **saturation**. Block's practical lever is the **warm/cool** split and the use of a controlled palette. Color affinity (a limited, harmonized palette) calms; color contrast (complementary hues, a single saturated accent in a desaturated field) intensifies and pulls the eye. The single saturated object in a muted frame is the strongest color-based attention tool.

**Movement.** Three kinds: movement *of the object*, movement *of the camera*, and movement *of the audience's eye* across the frame. Movement has direction and quality just like line. Direction of movement relative to screen edges and relative to other movement creates contrast or affinity. Movement toward/away (into deep space) is more intense than lateral movement across a flat plane.

**Rhythm.** The pattern formed by the repetition of any component over space (within a frame — repeated shapes, alternating tones) or over time (the cadence of cuts, the recurrence of a motif). **Alternating, even, and progressive** rhythms create different feels. Rhythmic contrast (breaking an established pattern) is a strong intensity spike. Editing rhythm — the durations of shots — is rhythm in time, which is where this component overlaps with Murch.

### Story structure ↔ Visual structure

Block's payoff chapter. The relationship between the two structures can be:

- **Support** — visual intensity curve runs *parallel* to the story's emotional curve. Most common, most invisible, most reliable.
- **Counterpoint** — visuals deliberately *contradict* the story (serene visuals over horror; visual chaos over a banal line). Powerful but must be a *choice*, used for irony or unease.

The unforgivable failure is the **accidental** mismatch: a flat, low-contrast, static visual treatment laid over a story beat that is supposed to be the climax. The audience's body says "this isn't important" while the words say "this is the most important thing." Block's discipline — graph your story's intensity, then graph your visual intensity, then make the second deliberately relate to the first — is the antidote.

### What Block gives the Remotion explainer maker

- A **diagnostic vocabulary**: when a scene reads as "slide," name *which component is flat*. Usually it's space (everything on one plane) and movement (everything static or linearly sliding) and rhythm (every shot the same length).
- A **build tool**: design a visual-intensity *progression* across an act. Open space, increase tonal/color contrast, accelerate and complicate movement, and tighten cut rhythm as the argument climaxes — then release into affinity at resolution.
- The **dominant contrast** as a per-frame attention discipline: every frame should have exactly one place the eye goes first, and it should be the place that carries the meaning. Static-image assets make this easy to neglect (the whole image competes for attention); Block forces you to subordinate everything to one focal point per beat, via tone, color, or motion.
- A reframing of static assets: stunning stills are *flat space by default*. Layering them into fore/mid/background, adding parallax, and animating a saturated accent against a muted field converts a flat slide into deep, intensity-controllable space.

## 2. Walter Murch — *In the Blink of an Eye*

### The core problem it solves

A cut is a discontinuity — one instant you're here, the next you're somewhere else. It *should* be jarring, yet it isn't. Murch's book is a meditation on *why cuts work at all*, and from that, *when* to make them. For the explainer maker, every Sequence boundary, every scene change, every shot swap is a cut, and most "PPT" video treats cuts as mere slide-advances (uniform, mechanical, on-the-beat-of-the-paragraph). Murch supplies the logic that makes a cut feel inevitable rather than administrative.

### Why a cut works: the blink

Murch's central metaphor. We imagine we see the world as a continuous take, but we don't — we **blink**, and the blink is a cut. More importantly, blinks are not random: we blink to *punctuate thought*. A blink happens at the moment a thought completes or a new one begins. To withhold a blink is intense concentration; rapid blinking is agitation; rhythmic blinking is the visible evidence of the brain "chewing" — separating one idea from the next.

The consequence: **a cut works when it happens at the point the audience is *already* ready to "blink"** — the moment they've finished absorbing one idea and are turning to the next. Cut there and the edit is invisible and right; the audience was going to make that mental break anyway. Cut against it and you fight the viewer's own cognition. Murch claims a good actor blinks where the cut should fall, and the editor can read performance rhythm off the blinks. For explainer work: cut where the *narration's idea* completes, not where the timeline grid says.

### The Rule of Six

Murch's most cited contribution: the six criteria of an ideal cut, ranked by importance, with (semi-tongue-in-cheek) percentages. An ideal cut satisfies all six at once; when you can't, **sacrifice from the bottom up, never the top**.

| Rank | Criterion | Weight | What it means |
|------|-----------|--------|---------------|
| 1 | **Emotion** | 51% | Does the cut preserve/serve the feeling the audience should have at this moment? |
| 2 | **Story** | 23% | Does the cut advance the narrative/argument? |
| 3 | **Rhythm** | 10% | Is the cut at a point that is rhythmically interesting and "right"? |
| 4 | **Eye-trace** | 7% | Does it respect where the audience's focus is in the frame, and where it moves to? |
| 5 | **Two-dimensional plane (planarity)** | 5% | Does it respect the grammar of screen direction — stage-line / 180° rule? |
| 6 | **Three-dimensional continuity** | 4% | Are people/objects where they were in the actual space? |

The radical claim: **emotion alone (51%) outweighs the other five combined.** If a cut delivers the right feeling, the audience will forgive — won't even notice — a continuity error, a crossed line, a rhythmic bump. Inverted: a technically flawless cut that betrays the emotion is a bad cut. Murch insists the *order* matters far more than the exact numbers; the numbers just dramatize how lopsided the top-vs-bottom split is.

He also notes each criterion tends to *mask* problems in the ones below it, but never the reverse — get emotion right and lower-order flaws hide; get only continuity right and the missing emotion is glaring.

### Dimensionality of the cut, and "wine" vs. eating

Murch frames editing as the management of discontinuity, and likens the editor's relationship to footage as something closer to wine-making than carpentry — you work *with* an organic material's own tendencies, sampling and adjusting, rather than forcing joints. Cuts, dissolves, and the decision of *how* discontinuous to be (hard cut vs. dissolve vs. fade) are tools for controlling how much the audience feels the seam.

### Practical maxims scattered through the book

- **"Dimensionality"** — a hard cut is a complete, instantaneous displacement; a dissolve is a *partial, simultaneous* presence of two images, useful to soften a time/space jump or signal interiority. Choose the seam's hardness to match how big a mental jump you're asking for.
- **Misdirection and eye-trace** — guide the viewer's eye to a specific zone *before* the cut, then place the corresponding point of interest in the same zone after the cut, and the cut becomes seamless. Move the eye where you want it, cut while it's "in transit."
- **Cut for the audience's process, not the director's convenience** — the cut serves the viewer's act of comprehension.
- **The "below decks" idea** — much of editing's effect operates beneath conscious awareness; the audience experiences the *result* (felt continuity, correct emotion) without seeing the mechanism.

### What Murch gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **A cut-placement test.** Before placing a scene boundary, ask the Rule of Six in order. In explainer video, "emotion" = the intended feeling of the beat (curiosity, tension of an unanswered question, the relief of a reveal), and "story" = does this cut move the argument forward. If a transition only satisfies rhythm (it's on the musical beat) but breaks the emotional/argumentative flow, it's wrong.
- **Cut on the idea, not the paragraph.** Use word-level narration timing to cut at the instant an idea *completes in the listener's mind* — the natural "blink" point — rather than at fixed durations. This is the single biggest upgrade from slide-pacing.
- **Eye-trace continuity across cuts.** Place the focal element of the outgoing frame and the incoming frame in compatible screen positions (or deliberately move the eye, then cut). With composed static assets you control focal position exactly — exploit it so the eye doesn't have to re-hunt after every cut.
- **Match seam-hardness to mental-jump-size.** Same-topic beat → hard cut. Big conceptual or temporal leap → dissolve/fade to flag the jump. Don't cross-dissolve everything (the mushy default) and don't hard-cut across a major shift (jarring).
- **Permission to break continuity for feeling.** If the emotionally right image requires breaking spatial logic, Murch says take it — the feeling wins.

## 3. Richard Williams — *The Animator's Survival Kit* + Thomas & Johnston — *The Illusion of Life*

These two are paired because they cover the same territory from two angles. **Thomas & Johnston** (two of Disney's "Nine Old Men," 1981) give the *philosophy and the canonical 12 principles* — the *why*. **Williams** (Oscar-winning director of animation on *Who Framed Roger Rabbit*, distilling his masterclasses) gives the *mechanics* — the *how*, frame by frame. For motion-graphics, the principles matter more than character draftsmanship: they're what separate "the card slides in" from "the card *arrives with weight.*"

### The foundational pair: Timing and Spacing

Williams' deepest point, and the one most directly applicable to Remotion: **timing and spacing are different things, and spacing is the hard part.**

- **Timing** = *how long* an action takes — the number of frames it occupies. It sets where the "accents," "beats," or "hits" land (the bouncing ball's "boinks" on the ground).
- **Spacing** = *how the positions are distributed* across those frames — the distance between successive drawings. Clustered drawings = slow movement; far-apart drawings = fast movement.

The classic demo: move a coin across the screen in one second (24 frames). Even spacing = mechanical, lifeless glide. Same one-second *timing*, but with the positions bunched at the start and end and spread in the middle = the coin eases out, races, eases in — and suddenly it feels *alive*. **Same timing, different spacing, completely different life.** Williams: even if the drawings were by Michelangelo, bad timing and spacing would still kill them. This is *exactly* the lever an interpolation curve controls — linear easing is "even spacing," and it is the #1 cause of "PPT motion."

### Keys, Extremes, Breakdowns, Inbetweens

The vocabulary of how a movement is built:

- **Keys / Key poses** — the essential storytelling positions; the poses that carry the meaning.
- **Extremes** — the furthest points of an action (the extreme ends).
- **Breakdown** — the pose *between* two keys that defines *how* you get from one to the other — the shape of the transition, the arc, the line of action mid-move. Williams: the breakdown is one of the most important decisions in the whole move. Its *position in time* controls tempo (closer to key A = slow out of A; closer to key B = slow into B; dead center = mechanical), and its *position in space* controls the path/arc. These two jobs are independent and you tune them separately.
- **Inbetweens** — the drawings that fill the remaining gaps. In 3D/Remotion, the software auto-interpolates the inbetweens, and "it's almost never what you want" — which is precisely why you must place breakdowns (extra keyframes) to force the spacing where you want it.

### Three ways to animate

1. **Pose to pose** — plan the key poses first, then fill in. Maximum control and clarity; can be stiff.
2. **Straight ahead** — animate frame by frame from the start, discovering as you go. Maximum spontaneity and fluidity; can drift out of control.
3. **The hybrid (Williams' recommendation)** — block in with pose-to-pose for structure and readability, then loosen with straight-ahead passes for life. The principle generalizes: *structure first, then organic refinement.*

### The 12 Principles of Animation (Thomas & Johnston)

The canonical list. These are the operating system of believable motion:

1. **Squash and Stretch** — the most fundamental. Objects deform to convey weight and flexibility while conserving volume (a squashed ball widens). Rigid = lifeless; squash/stretch = alive and physical. The amount signals material (a bowling ball barely deforms; a rubber ball deforms a lot).
2. **Anticipation** — a preparatory movement in the *opposite* direction before the main action (crouch before a jump, wind-up before a throw). It cues the audience to *expect* the action so they don't miss it, and it builds energy. Williams adds **surprise anticipations** and **invisible anticipations** (so subtle they aren't consciously seen but still register).
3. **Staging** — presenting an idea so it is "completely and unmistakably clear." Direct the eye to exactly one thing at a time via placement, light/shadow, camera angle, silhouette. Same lineage as Block's dominant contrast and Murch's eye-trace. Avoid unnecessary detail competing with the point.
4. **Straight Ahead & Pose to Pose** — the two methods above, named as a principle.
5. **Follow Through & Overlapping Action** — loose parts keep moving after the main mass stops (follow-through), and different parts start/stop at different times rather than all together (overlap). This is "drag" — antennae, hair, cloth, a trailing limb. It's what makes motion read as connected mass rather than a rigid block. Williams details **breaking joints**, **delaying parts**, and the **wave/whip** action that propagates down a flexible chain.
6. **Slow In and Slow Out (Ease)** — natural movement accelerates and decelerates rather than starting/stopping abruptly. (This is spacing: bunched frames at the ends.) The default of good motion — but Williams warns against *over*-easing everything into mush; sometimes you want a sharp, linear, or even snapped move.
7. **Arcs** — living things move on curved paths, not straight lines. Williams: "watch your arcs" — a hand, a head turn, a thrown object all travel arcs; straight-line interpolation between two poses is a classic beginner mistake that reads as robotic.
8. **Secondary Action** — a supporting action that enriches the main one (a facial expression accompanying a gesture, a coat swaying during a walk). It adds dimension *without competing* with the primary action.
9. **Timing** — the number of frames an action takes; determines weight (heavy things move slower, take more frames to start/stop) and meaning/emotion. The same pose held longer or shorter changes everything.
10. **Exaggeration** — push the action past literal reality to make it read and to give it appeal/energy. Caricature the essence rather than copying life flatly. Realism alone is often "stiff and dull."
11. **Solid Drawing** — drawing in true 3D space with weight, volume, and balance; avoiding "twins" (lifeless symmetry where left and right mirror exactly). In motion-graphics terms: respect dimensionality, weight, and asymmetry; don't let elements sit in dead, mirrored balance.
12. **Appeal** — the charisma/readability of what's on screen; not mere prettiness but a clear, engaging design the eye *wants* to look at. Simplicity, clear silhouette, and avoidance of clutter all serve appeal.

### Williams-specific craft beyond the 12

- **The bouncing ball "says it all"** — the master exercise; carries timing, spacing, squash/stretch, ease, and arcs in one simple form.
- **Walks** as the core test — weight, tempo, the **passing position** (the walk's breakdown), the **double bounce**, weight shift, the belt-line, counteraction of arms. The walk is the proving ground for weight and personality.
- **The moving hold** — a "hold" should never be truly frozen; even a held pose keeps drifting/settling slightly, or it dies on screen. (Directly relevant: a static asset sitting perfectly still reads as dead — give it a slow drift, breath, or settle.)
- **On ones vs. on twos** — exposing a new drawing every frame (ones) vs. every other frame (twos); a tempo/texture choice.
- **Classic inbetween mistakes** and **the elongated inbetween** — how naive midpointing destroys arcs and spacing.

### What this pair gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **The diagnosis of dead motion**: linear interpolation = even spacing = no life. The fix is spring physics or eased/custom curves that bunch frames at the ends (slow in / slow out), plus deliberately *uneven* spacing for snap.
- **Anticipation + follow-through on every entrance/exit.** Don't just fade a card in. Have it dip back slightly (anticipation), arrive with a slight overshoot, and settle (follow-through). Have trailing elements lag the leader (overlap). This is the difference between "placed" and "alive."
- **Arcs over straight lines.** Move elements along curved paths, not point-A-to-point-B lines.
- **Staging = one clear idea per beat**, reinforcing Block and Murch from the animation side.
- **The moving hold** for your stunning static assets: never let a held image be perfectly frozen — a slow push, parallax drift, or micro-scale keeps it breathing.
- **Weight via timing**: heavy/important things accelerate slowly and take more frames; light things snap. Mismatched weight (everything moving at the same uniform speed) is a core PPT tell.
- **Structure-then-loosen** workflow: block the scene's key poses/beats first, then add the organic secondary motion, overlap, and easing.

## 4. Scott McCloud — *Understanding Comics*

### The core problem it solves

Comics are *static images in deliberate sequence* — which is exactly what an explainer built from still assets is, before motion is added. McCloud's book is the deepest existing analysis of how a reader's mind turns a row of frozen pictures into time, motion, and meaning. His central insight directly dissolves the "islands of slides" problem: the meaning of a sequence lives **not in the panels but in the gaps between them**, and in the mind of the viewer who bridges those gaps.

McCloud's working definition of comics: *"juxtaposed pictorial and other images in deliberate sequence."* Swap "panels in a row" for "shots in a timeline" and the entire framework transfers.

### Closure — the engine of the whole medium

**Closure** is the mental act of observing the parts but perceiving the whole — filling in what isn't shown. In comics, between every two panels there is a **gutter** (the gap), and in that gutter *nothing is drawn* — yet the reader supplies the connecting action, the passage of time, the motion. McCloud's famous example: one panel shows a raised axe, the next shows a scream over a city skyline — *the reader commits the murder*, not the artist. The medium is built on what it *doesn't* show.

The consequence for sequenced stills: **the viewer is an active collaborator who bridges your cuts.** You don't have to show everything (the PPT instinct — fully explicit slides that leave nothing to infer). The participation *is* the engagement. Leave the right gap and the viewer's own mind generates motion and continuity for free — which is more involving than spelling it out.

### The six panel-to-panel transitions

McCloud's taxonomy of *how* one panel relates to the next — i.e., what kind of gap the reader must close. This is the most directly useful list for sequencing shots:

1. **Moment-to-moment** — a single action broken into adjacent beats; minimal closure (hand reaching → doorknob turning). Slows time down, adds weight/duration.
2. **Action-to-action** — one subject moves to a distinct next action (batter swings → ball is gone). The workhorse; advances events efficiently.
3. **Subject-to-subject** — staying in one scene/idea but shifting *what* we look at (a speaker → the listener's face). Requires more reader involvement; this is "coverage."
4. **Scene-to-scene** — a significant jump across time or space (walks into building → meeting an hour later). Demands deductive closure; the big leap.
5. **Aspect-to-aspect** — no time passes; the "camera" wanders across details of a place or mood (rain on a window, the cat, the half-empty mug). Builds atmosphere/mood rather than advancing events. Characteristic of contemplative, environmental storytelling.
6. **Non-sequitur** — no logical relationship between panels. Pure juxtaposition.

McCloud's data point: mainstream Western comics live almost entirely in transitions **2, 3, and 4** (action, subject, scene) — the "getting things done" transitions — and rarely use **1 (moment)** or **5 (aspect)**. **The diagnosis for "flat" sequencing: everything is action-to-action — each frame advances the argument by exactly one beat, like a checklist of events.** The cure is to deliberately deploy moment-to-moment (slow down, let a beat breathe, two frames on one small change) and aspect-to-aspect (wander the mood of a scene without advancing) to vary the texture. This maps almost one-to-one onto Block's density rhythm and Murch's cut logic.

### Time in a frame, motion in stillness

McCloud devotes a chapter to how comics *portray time*:

- A single panel is usually read as a single *moment* — but it isn't truly instantaneous. The presence of words, multiple speech balloons, or several actions inside one frame *stretches* its perceived duration (a panel with three balloons takes "longer" than a silent one).
- **Panel shape and width** alter perceived time — a long horizontal panel reads as a longer duration than a square one, even with identical content.
- **The gutter's width / the number of panels** controls pacing: more panels covering the same action = slower, more deliberate; fewer = faster.
- **Motion** in a still is created by closure between frames, and within a frame by motion lines, streaking, multiple-image blur, and (modern) the "camera blur" of background. The mind reconstructs the movement the static image only implies.

For Remotion: a held frame's duration, the "width" you give a beat in time, and how many intermediate shots you use to cover an action are all *pacing controls* — the timeline analog of panel size and gutter count.

### Word–picture combinations

McCloud's taxonomy of how text and image interact — critical for narrated explainer video, where voiceover (words) plays over imagery (pictures):

1. **Word-specific** — words carry the meaning; pictures merely illustrate.
2. **Picture-specific** — pictures carry it; words are a "soundtrack."
3. **Duo-specific** — words and pictures say the *same* thing (redundant).
4. **Additive** — one amplifies or elaborates the other.
5. **Parallel** — words and pictures follow non-intersecting tracks.
6. **Montage** — words treated as integral pictorial elements.
7. **Interdependent (the goal)** — words and pictures *together* convey an idea **neither could alone**; each does work the other can't. McCloud's prized mode (he likens it to haiku — the whole exceeds the juxtaposed parts).

The failure mode for explainer video is **duo-specific**: the narration says "the bridge collapsed" and the image shows a bridge collapsing — the visual is pure redundancy, adding zero. This is a deep cause of "elegant PPT": the picture only re-states the words. The fix is **interdependent** design — the image should carry a *dimension the narration doesn't* (the emotional weight, the scale, the mechanism, the consequence), so that muting either channel loses meaning. (This is the same instinct as the "complementarity test" — mute the audio, or close your eyes: if either alone is sufficient, the other is wasted.)

### The six steps of creation (the "apple")

McCloud's model of any work's layers, core to surface — useful as a priorities ladder:

1. **Idea/Purpose** — the content, the *why*, what the work is meant to say. (Deepest.)
2. **Form** — the medium chosen (a comic, a video, a poem).
3. **Idiom** — the genre/school/vocabulary it speaks in.
4. **Structure** — what to include and omit; composition, pacing, arrangement.
5. **Craft** — the skill of construction; problem-solving, technique.
6. **Surface** — production values; what the audience perceives *first*. (Outermost.)

McCloud's point: beginners work from the *surface inward* (polish first), masters work from the *core outward* (purpose first). The relevant warning for your situation: **stunning assets are surface (step 6). If the work feels like elegant PPT, the deficiency is almost always at structure (step 4) — sequence, pacing, omission — not at surface.** You've nailed the outermost layer; the PPT feeling comes from an under-designed structure layer.

### What McCloud gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **Permission and instruction to leave gaps.** Don't render every beat explicit. Trust closure — show A and C, let the viewer build B. Engagement comes from participation.
- **A transition palette beyond "next slide."** Audit your sequence: if every shot is action-to-action, inject moment-to-moment (slow, weighty beats) and aspect-to-aspect (mood/atmosphere wandering) for rhythm.
- **Pacing via duration and shot-count**, the timeline equivalent of panel width and gutter density.
- **The interdependent word/picture target.** The strongest defense against PPT: make every image carry a dimension the narration doesn't. If muting the voiceover *or* hiding the image each loses real meaning, you've escaped duo-specific redundancy.
- **The diagnosis ladder.** When it feels like PPT, suspect *structure* (step 4), not *surface* (step 6) — you already have the surface.

## Synthesis — the four books as one toolkit

Each book attacks a different axis of the same problem. Together they form a layered diagnostic for "why does this feel like elegant PPT?"

| Book | Axis it owns | The PPT failure it names | The lever it hands you |
|------|--------------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| **Block** | Visual structure *over time* | Flat visual-intensity line; relentlessly flat space; uniform rhythm | Contrast/affinity progression; dominant contrast; deep space |
| **Murch** | The *cut* | Cuts as mechanical slide-advances on the paragraph grid | Rule of Six; cut on the "blink"/idea-completion; eye-trace |
| **Williams / T&J** | *Motion* itself | Linear easing, uniform speed, frozen holds — "placed not alive" | Spacing vs timing; anticipation/follow-through/arcs; the moving hold |
| **McCloud** | *Sequence* of stills | Islands of slides; everything action-to-action; duo-specific redundancy | Closure; the six transitions; interdependent word/picture |

**The unifying thread** is that all four locate "aliveness" in *time and the viewer's mind*, not in the individual image. 3. **Motion and cuts must serve feeling, not the grid.** Murch's *emotion = 51%* = Disney's *emotional timing* = Block's *story↔visual coupling*. Time things to the idea and the feeling, never to a fixed slide interval.

The fastest visible wins, in order:
1. **Kill linear easing.** Replace `interpolate` linear ramps with spring physics or eased curves (slow-in/slow-out) and add small anticipation + overshoot to entrances/exits. (Williams)
