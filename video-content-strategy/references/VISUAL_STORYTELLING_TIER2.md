# Visual Storytelling — Tier 2 Reference

The staging, framing, and meaning layer. Where Tier 1 covered the four load-bearing axes (visual structure over time, the cut, motion, and sequence), Tier 2 zooms into a tighter question: **what goes inside a single frame, and how do you chain frames into a designed path for the eye?** These four books are about composition as storytelling — staging the frame so the eye lands where the meaning is, and so each frame talks to the next.

- **Marcos Mateu-Mestre, *Framed Ink*** — how to stage a single frame for emotion and clarity, then chain frames for continuity. The DreamWorks layout artist's system.
- **Molly Bang, *Picture This*** — the emotional grammar of pure shape, size, and placement. Tiny book, deepest figure-ground muscle.
- **David Mamet, *On Directing Film*** — the ruthless cut-to-meaning discipline. What shot do you actually *need*, and why.
- **Steven Katz, *Film Directing: Shot by Shot*** — the standard catalogue of staging, blocking, and shot design. How a sequence becomes a designed path.

Same structure as Tier 1: recollect the core content, then map each to Remotion + static-image work.

## 1. Marcos Mateu-Mestre — *Framed Ink*

### The core problem it solves

Mateu-Mestre is a feature-animation layout/visual-development artist (DreamWorks, Sony — *Prince of Egypt*, *Balto*, *Surf's Up*). *Framed Ink* is the bridge book between the comics tradition (Tier 1's McCloud) and the film tradition (Murch, Katz): it treats the comic panel and the movie screen as the same problem — *a frame that must carry story and emotion at once*. Its thesis, stated on the cover: make the audience **feel** the story while they're **reading** the story. The frame is not a container for information; it is an emotional instrument.

For the explainer maker, this is the antidote to the "informative but inert" frame — the slide that contains the right content but generates no feeling. Mateu-Mestre's whole system is about engineering the feeling *through* the composition, not in spite of it.

### The three-level structure of the book

1. **Composing a single image** — drama, focus, and emotion inside one frame.
2. **Composing steady shots** — how a held shot carries its beat.
3. **Composing for continuity** — how shots connect into a flowing sequence.

He then applies all three to three worked storylines (a train accident, a cowboy tale, bikers approaching a mysterious house), showing the same principles deployed across genres and moods.

### Composing the single image

The core levers Mateu-Mestre teaches for a single frame:

- **Complexity vs. simplicity** — a busy, complex frame vs. a clean, simple one. Each carries a different emotional charge; complexity = tension/chaos/overwhelm, simplicity = clarity/calm/focus. Match the level of visual complexity to the emotional content of the beat.
- **Light and dark (notan / value design)** — the single most powerful tool in his kit. Where the lights and darks fall *is* the composition. He designs in value thumbnails first: the placement of a bright shape against a dark field (or vice versa) directs the eye and sets mood before any detail exists. A lone light figure in a dark frame reads as isolation/vulnerability; a dark mass intruding into light reads as threat.
- **Size and distance** — how big the subject is in frame, and how near/far it reads, controls intimacy and power (close = intimate or threatening; distant = lonely, observed, small).
- **Point of view / camera angle** — low angle empowers the subject, high angle diminishes it; the chosen POV is an emotional statement, never neutral.
- **Leading the eye** — using line, perspective, value edges, and the direction of the staged action to route the viewer's gaze to the dramatic focal point, in a deliberate order. The frame should have a clear "first thing you see," then a path.

### The line of action and dynamic staging

Mateu-Mestre stresses a **dominant line of action** through the composition — a directional spine (often diagonal) that organizes the frame and carries energy. Diagonals create dynamism and instability; horizontals/verticals create stability. Tilting the frame's internal lines, staging along a diagonal, and opposing one line against another are how a static drawing acquires kinetic charge. (This rhymes precisely with Block's *line* component and Bang's *diagonal = movement*.)

### Continuity — the chapter that matters most

His most valued contribution, and the hardest part of visual storytelling. Continuity is *how consecutive frames flow into one another* so the sequence reads as a coherent dramatic progression rather than disconnected pictures. Key ideas:

- **Maintain a coherent screen direction and spatial logic** so the audience always knows where they are and how players relate — unless you break it deliberately for effect.
- **Vary shot size and angle between consecutive frames** to keep visual interest and to control emphasis (a cut from wide to close *means* something — it focuses attention or escalates).
- **Design the sequence as a rising-and-falling emotional line**, where each frame's composition (value, complexity, line of action) is chosen for its position in that arc — the visual equivalent of pacing.
- **The frame should change in a way that earns the cut** — echoing Murch, the change between frames is where meaning is generated.

### What *Framed Ink* gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **Value-first composition.** Before fussing detail, design each scene as a notan: where is the light, where is the dark, what single shape wins the eye? With composed static assets, you can dim/darken the periphery and let one lit element carry the beat — this is the substrate doctrine in practice.
- **Match complexity to emotion.** A chaotic beat earns a busy frame; a clarity beat earns a clean one. Don't run uniform visual complexity across every scene.
- **Stage a line of action.** Give each frame a directional spine (often diagonal) so even a still composition has kinetic charge — then your camera move or element entrance can ride that line.
- **Angle is never neutral.** Choose a "virtual camera" height/POV per beat for emotional reasons (looking down on the diminished thing, up at the dominant thing).
- **Design continuity as an emotional line.** Pick each scene's composition for its place in the act's rising/falling arc — the composition-side complement to Block's intensity progression.

## 2. Molly Bang — *Picture This: How Pictures Work*

### The core problem it solves

Bang's book is tiny, almost wordless, and profound. She builds the emotional content of "Little Red Riding Hood" out of nothing but **cut-paper shapes** — colored triangles, rectangles, circles — and in doing so isolates a question no other book asks so cleanly: *why does pure form, stripped of all representation, make us feel something?* A red triangle is not a wolf, yet arranged correctly it reads as menace. This is the deepest available training for figure-ground instinct and for understanding that emotion is carried by **structure, not subject**.

For the explainer maker drowning in "stunning assets," Bang is the corrective: the feeling doesn't come from the gorgeous render, it comes from the *shape, size, placement, and color relationships* underneath it — which means you can engineer feeling even with simple elements, and you can accidentally kill it even with beautiful ones.

### The principles (her derived list)

Bang arrives at these empirically, by building the Red Riding Hood picture and noticing what makes it work:

1. **Smooth, flat, horizontal shapes give a sense of stability and calm.** Horizontal areas are "islands of calm." (Associated with the horizon, with lying down, with rest.)
2. **Vertical shapes are more active and exciting.** They rebel against gravity, implying energy and reaching upward.
3. **Diagonal shapes and lines are dynamic** because they imply motion or tension (something falling, rising, or unstable). The single strongest static cue for movement.
4. **The upper half of the frame is freedom, happiness, triumph, spirituality** — and also, because it's "up," a place of more emotional "lightness." Objects high in the frame feel more powerful but also more precarious.
5. **The lower half feels heavier, sadder, more constrained, more grounded** — but also safer/more stable. (Up = aspiration and threat-of-falling; down = security and oppression.)
6. **The center of the page is the point of greatest attraction** — the most stable, most-attended position. Keeping the focus *off* center makes the eye explore and creates tension.
7. **Edges and the frame.** Objects at the edge or breaking the frame imply space and action *outside* the picture, creating tension and extension beyond what's shown.
8. **Pointed shapes feel scary; rounded shapes feel safe and comforting.** Spikes, teeth, jagged points = threat; curves, circles = security. (This is why Bang's wolf is built from sharp triangles.)
9. **The larger an object in the frame, the stronger and more dominant it feels.** Size = power. A small figure feels vulnerable.
10. **We associate same/similar colors more strongly than same/similar shapes.** Color is a stronger grouping cue than form — a red triangle and a red circle "belong together" more than two triangles of different colors. (This is why a single recurring color can thread meaning through a whole piece.)
11. **Color carries strong specific associations** — red = blood, danger, passion, life, heat, alarm (the most charged color); also warm vs. cool temperature feelings. Bang notes red feels more "alive" and urgent than other hues.
12. **Light vs. dark backgrounds.** Light backgrounds feel safer (human daytime vision); dark backgrounds feel more dangerous/uncertain (we see poorly at night).

### The two meta-principles beneath the list

- **Contrast enables perception.** "We notice contrasts; contrast is what enables us to see." Without contrast there is no information — the eye registers difference. (Same root principle as Block's contrast/affinity, arrived at independently.)
- **Regularity vs. irregularity.** In nature nothing is *perfectly* regular or *perfectly* chaotic. Either extreme — total order or total randomness — reads as threatening or lifeless. Emotional truth lives in the proportion between the two. A perfectly symmetrical, perfectly even composition feels dead (cf. Disney's "twins"); a totally chaotic one feels unbearable.
- **Exaggeration over realism for emotion.** "When we want a picture to feel scary, it's more effective to graphically exaggerate the scary aspects than to render them photographically — because that's the way we *feel* things look." (Cf. Disney's exaggeration principle.)

### What *Picture This* gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **Engineer feeling from structure, not subject.** Decide the emotional charge of a beat, then choose shape (pointed vs. round), placement (high/low, center/edge), size (dominant vs. vulnerable), and color *to match* — independent of how pretty the asset is.
- **The vertical axis is an emotion dial.** Put the thing that should feel triumphant/aspirational high in the frame; put the thing that should feel oppressed/grounded/safe low. Animate position changes to *move* a subject along this emotional axis.
- **Size = power, as a deliberate choice.** Scale the dominant idea large; scale the threatened/minor element small. Don't default everything to comfortable mid-size (a PPT tell).
- **Color as the strongest thread.** Use a single charged accent color (red for the stakes, the danger, the key term) to group and thread meaning across scenes — Bang says color binds more strongly than shape, so it's your best continuity-of-meaning tool.
- **Avoid dead symmetry.** Perfectly centered, perfectly balanced frames read as lifeless. Push focus off-center for tension and exploration; reserve dead-center for moments of maximal stability/finality.
- **Points vs. curves as a mood switch.** Build threatening beats from angular/jagged geometry, safe beats from rounded geometry — even in abstract motion-graphics overlays.

## 3. David Mamet — *On Directing Film*

### The core problem it solves

Mamet's book (≈106 pages, mostly Socratic dialogue from his Columbia film classes) is the most ruthless cure available for the show-everything instinct that produces PPT. His entire argument is an attack on the inert, illustrative, "follow the character around and hope something interesting happens" approach — which is *exactly* the failure mode of narrated slides where the image just illustrates the voiceover. Mamet replaces it with a brutally disciplined question-driven method: figure out what each unit is *about*, then find the simplest uninflected images whose **juxtaposition** creates that meaning in the audience's mind.

### Storytelling is the juxtaposition of uninflected images

Mamet's central claim, inherited from Eisenstein and Pudovkin (Soviet montage): **meaning is not in the shot, it's in the cut.** A film is "a succession of images juxtaposed so that the contrast between these images moves the story forward in the mind of the audience."

- **Uninflected images.** Each individual shot should be *neutral* — it should not strain to carry emotion, narration, or meaning through fancy camera work, lighting tricks, or "expressive" performance. The shot is a plain, declarative statement. The meaning emerges only when shots are *cut together*.
- His canonical example: don't show a man looking in a fridge, rubbing his stomach, voicing-over "I'm hungry." Instead cut: **shot of a man's face → shot of the inside of a refrigerator** → and the idea of *hunger* is created in the audience's mind. The two neutral images collide to produce a meaning that's in *neither* shot alone.
- He hears natural storytelling as already cinematic: "I'm on the corner. Foggy day. People running around. A car comes up..." — that's a shot list. People narrate in cuts.
- Mamet is "resolutely against following the protagonist around" (he dislikes the Steadicam-as-crutch) — because trailing a character is the lazy substitute for *designing* the juxtapositions that actually tell the story.

This is McCloud's *closure* and Murch's *cut-as-meaning*, stated as a directing discipline: trust the audience's mind to manufacture the meaning from two plain images.

### The objective: what does the protagonist want?

Mamet's method for finding *what to shoot*. Every unit of story is driven by an objective — a want.

> "What does the hero want? What hinders him from getting it? What happens if he does not get it?"

- **As long as the protagonist wants something, the audience wants something too.** Want is the engine of attention. Strip the want and the audience checks out — no matter how pretty the images.
- He distinguishes **want vs. need** (Dumbo *wants* to not have big ears; what he *needs* is to feel okay about himself). The surface objective drives the scene; the deeper need drives the film.
- The problem should be **rooted in character/situation**, not in spectacle.

### The structural hierarchy: throughline → scene → beat

Mamet decomposes the work top-down:

- **Throughline (super-objective)** — the spine of the whole film; what the protagonist wants across the entire story.
- **Scene** — a unit with its own objective that advances the throughline. "A formal essay." Each scene must be *about* one thing.
- **Beat** — the smallest unit; what a particular moment is about. You ask of every beat: *what is this about?* and find the uninflected images that convey it.

The method: for each scene, state its objective as a simple want; break it into beats; for each beat find the simplest sequence of plain shots whose juxtaposition conveys it. **Never ask "what would be interesting to show" — ask "what does this beat need to say, and what is the simplest image-pair that says it."**

### "Get in late, get out early" and the creation of order

- **Enter a scene as late as possible, leave as early as possible** — cut all the throat-clearing; include only what advances the objective.
- The dramatist's job is **not** to create chaos or confrontation but to **create order**: start with a disordering event and let the beat be the attempt to restore order. The scene ends when the objective is achieved or made permanently impossible — then cut.
- **Keep it simple and specific.** Mamet prizes economy: the plain, specific, uninflected shot over the ornate one. The discipline of "what do I cut to next, and why" over the indulgence of the beautiful lingering image.

### What *On Directing Film* gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **The "what is this beat about?" audit.** For every scene, force a one-sentence answer. If you can't state it, the scene is decoration — cut or redesign it. This is the sharpest possible weapon against pretty-but-pointless slides.
- **Juxtaposition over illustration.** Stop making the image *illustrate* the narration (duo-specific redundancy, in McCloud's terms). Instead, design image-pairs whose *collision* produces the idea — face + fridge = hunger. The meaning the viewer builds themselves is the engagement.
- **Give every segment a want.** Even an explainer has an objective per section — a question the viewer wants answered, a tension seeking resolution. Frame each act around a want so "the audience wants something too." A flat sequence of true statements has no want; that's why it feels like PPT.
- **Uninflected images = trust the cut.** You don't need every frame to be maximally dramatic and over-designed. Plain, clear images cut together with intent often beat a parade of lavish ones. (A useful counterweight to "my assets are stunning" — stunning-but-inert loses to plain-but-juxtaposed.)
- **Get in late, get out early.** Trim each scene to only the beats that serve its objective. Most "draggy" explainer sections are scenes entered too early and left too late.
- **Throughline → scene → beat** as the planning skeleton for an episode: one spine, scenes that each advance it, beats that each say one thing.

## 4. Steven Katz — *Film Directing: Shot by Shot*

### The core problem it solves

Katz's book (the best-selling film-directing text ever, 250k+ copies) is the comprehensive *catalogue* — the reference that names and diagrams the full vocabulary of shot design, staging, and coverage. Where Mamet gives you the philosophy (juxtaposition, objective) and Mateu-Mestre/Bang give you the single-frame emotion, Katz gives you the **engineering manual**: how to visualize a sequence before you make it, how to stage players in space, how to design the path of the eye across a whole scene, and what every shot type and camera move *means*. It's the book that turns "I have a vague sense of the scene" into "here is the precise shot list and blocking."

For the explainer maker, Katz is the systematic toolbox: the named shot sizes, angles, framings, and moves you can deploy deliberately instead of defaulting to the same flat full-shot every time (the PPT default).

### Visualization: the whole point of pre-planning

Part I is about **visualization** — designing the look and shot flow *before* production, via storyboards and shot lists. Katz's premise: directing is fundamentally *thinking visually in advance*. The storyboard is the tool for solving spatial and editing problems on paper (cheap) rather than on set (expensive). For Remotion, this maps exactly: design the shot sequence and staging as boards/thumbnails before building compositions — the timeline is your set, and rework is costly.

### The A, I, and L patterns — his signature contribution

Katz's tool for taming "the hundreds of staging choices facing a director in every scene." He reduces complex blocking to three basic **staging patterns** based on where players and camera sit relative to the **line of action**:

- **A pattern** — players staged at an angle / apex arrangement (triangular), camera working the apex.
- **I pattern** — players staged in a straight line, frontal or in-line with the camera axis (a head-on, "in line" relationship).
- **L pattern** — players staged at right angles to each other, forming an L in the staging space.

The point isn't the letters; it's that **most staging reduces to a few repeatable geometric templates**, and once you see the pattern you can systematically generate coverage (the shot options) from it. It's a compression scheme for blocking decisions.

### Elements of the continuity style

Part II is the grammar of classical (invisible) continuity — the system that lets an audience read cut-together shots as a coherent space and time:

- **Spatial connections (composing shots).** How to compose shots so consecutive frames connect into a believable, consistent space.
- **The line of action / the 180° rule.** The imaginary line through the action; keep the camera on one side so screen direction stays consistent (player A always frame-left, B always frame-right). Crossing it disorients — Murch's "planarity." Katz teaches both how to obey it and how to cross it deliberately (via a neutral shot, a moving camera, etc.).
- **Editing: temporal connections.** Matching action across cuts, cutting on motion, editing patterns that preserve continuity of time.
- **Sight lines / eyeline match.** Where players look ties shots together; the eyeline tells the audience the spatial relationship.

### The catalogue of shots, angles, framings, moves

Part III–IV are the deep reference. The vocabulary every explainer maker should be able to deploy on purpose:

- **Shot sizes** — extreme long / establishing, long/full shot, medium, close-up, extreme close-up. Each shot size is an emphasis decision: the close-up *forces* attention and intimacy; the wide *establishes* context and relationship. Cutting between sizes is how you control where the audience's interest sits.
- **Camera angles** — eye-level (neutral), high angle (subject diminished), low angle (subject empowered), bird's-eye. Angle is editorial — it states an attitude toward the subject.
- **Open vs. closed framing** — open framings imply a world continuing beyond the frame edges (the frame is a window); closed framings feel self-contained and composed (the frame is a box). A control for whether the scene feels expansive or contained/claustrophobic.
- **Depth of the frame** — staging in **foreground / midground / background** to create depth and to relate elements across planes. The opposite of flat slide-space; the explicit cure for "everything on one plane."
- **Point of view** — objective (observer) vs. subjective (we see *as* a character sees). POV shots bind the audience to a perspective.
- **The moving camera** — the **pan** (pivot), the **crane/boom** (vertical/sweeping reveal), the **tracking/dolly shot** (camera travels with or past the subject), and **tracking-shot choreography** (designing camera + subject motion together). Each move has a meaning: a push-in intensifies/commits; a pull-out reveals context or abandons; a track alongside accompanies; a crane-up grants overview or release.
- **Transitions** — how scenes hand off (cut, dissolve, wipe, and motivated transitions), the sequence-level connective tissue.

### What *Shot by Shot* gives the Remotion explainer maker

- **A real shot vocabulary to vary.** Stop defaulting to the flat full-shot per scene. Deliberately sequence establishing → medium → close to control emphasis and rhythm. The close-up is your "this is the important detail" tool; the wide is your "here's the context" tool.
- **Storyboard before you build.** Thumbnail the shot flow and staging of an act before writing composition code — solve eye-path and continuity problems on paper, where they're cheap.
- **Stage in depth (FG/MG/BG).** Katz's depth-of-frame is the direct technical cure for PPT-flatness: separate your static assets into foreground/midground/background planes and stage relationships across them (also enabling parallax).
- **Respect (and deliberately break) the line of action.** Keep screen direction consistent across cuts so the viewer never has to re-orient; cross the line only on purpose, with a motivated/neutral move. This is the spatial-continuity layer Murch ranks lower but that still quietly sells coherence.
- **Camera moves have meaning — choose accordingly.** Push-in to commit/intensify, pull-out to reveal/contextualize, track to accompany, crane to grant overview. Map each move to the beat's intent rather than adding motion for motion's sake.
- **Open vs. closed framing as a mood control.** Box the frame for contained/tense beats; open it for expansive/free beats — pairs with Bang's edge/frame principle.
- **A,I,L as a composition-staging shortcut.** When arranging multiple elements (two concepts in dialogue, three forces in tension), reach for a repeatable geometric template rather than improvising placement each time.

## Synthesis — Tier 2 as the staging-and-meaning layer

Tier 1 owned the macro axes (structure over time, the cut, motion, sequence). Tier 2 operates one level down: **inside the frame, and across adjacent frames.** Three of the four converge on a single discipline — *compose the frame so the eye lands on the meaning* — approached from different angles; the fourth (Mamet) attacks *what to put in the frame at all.*

| Book | Scope | The PPT failure it names | The lever it hands you |
|------|-------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| **Framed Ink** | Single frame → continuity | Informative but inert frames; no feeling | Value/notan design; line of action; complexity-to-emotion match |
| **Picture This** | Pure form & emotion | Feeling-dead composition under pretty assets | Shape/size/placement/color as emotion dials |
| **On Directing Film** | What to shoot, and why | Illustrative, want-less, show-everything slides | "What is this beat about?"; juxtaposition; objective |
| **Shot by Shot** | Staging & coverage catalogue | The same flat full-shot every scene | Shot-size/angle/framing/move vocabulary; depth; storyboarding |

**The convergent commands, restated for Tier 2:**

1. **Emotion is built, not found.** Bang and Mateu-Mestre both insist feeling comes from *structure* (shape, value, size, placement, line) — not from the subject or the render quality. You can engineer it deliberately; you can also kill it with a beautiful-but-dead composition.
2. **The frame is an argument, not a container.** Every frame should have a clear focal point, a path for the eye, and a reason to feel a certain way (Mateu-Mestre's leading-the-eye, Katz's shot-size emphasis, Bang's center/edge tension).
3. **Meaning lives between frames; trust juxtaposition.** Mamet's face+fridge = McCloud's closure = Murch's cut. Stop illustrating the narration; design collisions that make the viewer build the meaning.
4. **Vary the staging vocabulary; nothing is neutral.** Katz's shot sizes, angles, framings, and moves are all editorial. Uniform staging (one flat shot size, one neutral angle, no depth) is the visual signature of PPT. Depth (FG/MG/BG) is the direct cure for flatness.

**How Tier 2 plugs into Tier 1:**

- Bang and Mateu-Mestre are the *single-frame* implementation of Block's contrast/affinity and dominant-contrast (which component, where, to what emotional end).
- Mamet is the *directing-discipline* version of McCloud's closure and Murch's cut — and supplies the "want/objective" engine that pure visual theory doesn't.
- Katz is the *coverage and staging catalogue* that operationalizes Murch's continuity criteria (line of action, eye-trace) and gives Block's "deep space" its concrete technique (depth of frame, FG/MG/BG).

### Concrete next move for the Remotion pipeline (Tier 2 additions)

Layering onto the Tier 1 next-moves:
1. **Notan-thumbnail each scene** (value-first): where's the light, where's the dark, what one shape wins the eye? Dim the periphery so the focal element reads. (Framed Ink)
2. **Assign each beat an emotion → set shape/size/placement/color to match** (pointed vs round, high vs low, large vs small, charged accent color). (Picture This)
3. **Run the "what is this beat about?" audit** on every scene; cut or redesign any that only illustrate the narration; design juxtaposition-pairs instead. (Mamet)
4. **Give each act a want** (a question/tension the viewer wants resolved) so attention has an engine. (Mamet)
5. **Build a shot-size/angle/move plan per act** — vary establishing/medium/close, choose angles and camera moves for meaning, stage assets in FG/MG/BG for depth. Storyboard before building. (Katz)
6. **Lock screen direction** across cuts (line of action) so the viewer never re-orients unless you intend it. (Katz)

Together with Tier 1, these turn the four-book theory into a per-scene checklist: *state the want and the beat-meaning (Mamet), thumbnail the value and emotion (Framed Ink + Bang), choose the shot/angle/depth/move (Katz), then animate with intent and cut on the idea (Tier 1: Williams + Murch), tracking the intensity progression across the act (Block) and varying the transitions (McCloud).*
