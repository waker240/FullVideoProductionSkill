# Visual Storytelling — the film-craft layer

A second lens on the same target as [`cinematic-direction.md`](./cinematic-direction.md). That file argues from **cognitive science** (bandwidth, working memory, the awe of a managed prediction-error). This file argues from the **traditional craft canon** — film editing, cinematography, animation, comics, and story structure. They converge on the same commands from different vocabularies; where they converge, believe it harder. Where this file is *additive*, it supplies concrete tools the doctrine lacks: a visual-intensity curve, a cut-decision test, the physics of alive motion, a shape-to-emotion grammar, and the value-turn audit.

**Read this when** a piece feels like "elegant slides" — informative, competent, well-rendered, and inert. That feeling is almost never a *surface* problem (your assets are fine); it's a deficit in the **temporal and relational layers**: how intensity builds across a sequence, how one shot becomes the next, how things move, how the gaps are bridged, and whether anything *turns*. This reference is self-contained; the three project-root `VISUAL_STORYTELLING_TIER*.md` docs are optional deeper enrichment.

Apply these as diagnostic lenses to the current brief. A simple visual can hold a relationship while narration advances the explanation; a reference frame, qualification or requested big-text comparison need not perform a separate dramatic reversal. “PPT” is a complaint about an inert whole-film treatment, not a prohibition on an individual readable slide-like shot. For reviewed projects, [reference-led-direction.md](reference-led-direction.md) owns the interpretation of those requests.

> The through-line of the entire canon, in one sentence (Murch/Welles): **the film is in the *durations* of the images and *that which follows each image* — not in the images themselves.** Stunning stills are raw material. The video *happens* in the sequencing. Internalize this and you stop over-investing the assets and start designing time.

---

## The five convergent commands

Every author below, in their own vocabulary, issues these. If you do only five things, do these.

1. **One clear focus per beat.** Block's *dominant contrast* = Murch's *eye-trace* = Disney's *staging* = Mascelli's *single center of interest* = Mateu-Mestre's *leading the eye*. Subordinate everything in the frame to the one thing that carries the meaning *now*. (This is the awe layer's 120% element, from the composition side.)
2. **Vary the rhythm, and make it build.** Block's *intensity progression* = Murch's *rhythm* = Williams' *timing* = McCloud's *transition variety* = McKee's *nested escalation*. Uniform pacing is the master signature of slides.
3. **Motion and cuts serve feeling, not the grid.** Murch's *emotion = 51%* = Disney's *emotional timing* = Block's *story↔visual coupling*. Time things to the idea and the feeling, never to a fixed interval.
4. **Let the viewer do work.** McCloud's *closure* = Mamet's *juxtaposition* = Murch's *below-decks* invisible edit. Don't over-explain; the gap between two images, bridged by the viewer's mind, is where engagement lives.
5. **Every unit must have a job.** McKee's *value-turn*, Mascelli's *contrasting shots* and Murch's *organically-true cut* help diagnose an inert sequence. A scene may change an argument-state or deliberately hold it for reading, orientation or synthesis. Cut or redesign when it contributes neither.

## Layer 1 — STRUCTURE (the skeleton: McKee + Mamet)

Your explainers are arguments, and an argument has the same load-bearing structure as a story. The failure at this layer is *the list*: true, well-illustrated statements with no spine, no want, no escalation, no turn.

- **Controlling idea = your thesis, in one sentence: [value] + [cause].** "X happens *because* Y." Pin it; every act must build toward or test it. A beautiful scene that doesn't serve it gets cut. (Same as the scripting layer's "thesis before topics.")
- **The value-turn audit.** Ask how the viewer's understanding changes: confusion→clarity, doubt→conviction, question→answer. A scene can support that change through narration over a stable comparison; it need not reverse the image's emotional charge. If an interval neither advances understanding nor provides needed reading/orientation/release, cut or redesign it.
- **The gap (the engagement engine).** Set up an expectation, then violate it — "I expected X, but it's actually Y." The gap between expectation and result is where the viewer's schema updates, which is where learning and attention happen. This is the structural form of the awe test's *managed prediction-error*. A confirming-only explainer never opens a gap; it's inert.
- **Theme / anti-theme alternation (earns the conclusion).** Don't march straight to the thesis. Raise the counter-argument, let it win a round, then overturn it. The oscillation is what makes the final claim land as *proven* rather than *asserted*.
- **"What is this beat about?" (Mamet).** Force a one-sentence answer for every scene. If you can't state it, the scene is decoration. Then find the *simplest* image-pair whose juxtaposition conveys it — don't illustrate, collide (a face + an open fridge = hunger; the meaning is in neither shot alone).
- **Give every segment a want.** Even an explainer has an objective per section — a question the viewer wants answered, a tension seeking resolution. As long as the segment wants something, the viewer wants something too. A flat sequence of true statements has no want.
- **Get in late, get out early.** Trim each scene to only the beats that serve its objective. Most draggy sections are entered too early and left too late.
- **Nested escalation.** beat → scene → sequence → act, each climaxing harder than the level below. This is the *cause* of the rising visual-intensity curve in Layer 2 — the visuals rise because the *stakes* rise.

The explainer arc, mapped: **hook** (inciting question that throws understanding out of balance) → **escalating complications** (each section deepens or complicates the idea, alternating with counter-arguments) → **crisis** (the hardest version of the question) → **climax** (the decisive reframe that proves the thesis) → **resolution** (the brief settling, the implication).

## Layer 2 — VISUAL STRUCTURE OVER TIME (Block)

Most makers structure the *story* carefully but leave the *visual* track to instinct, so it runs flat while the argument tries to build. Treat visual structure as a designed parameter.

**The master principle — Contrast & Affinity:** contrast (visual difference) *increases* intensity; affinity (similarity) *decreases* it. This governs all seven visual components (space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, rhythm). Bright-against-dark, warm-against-cool, diagonal-against-flat, fast-against-still — all crank intensity up.

**The build tool — design an intensity progression per act.** Graph the argument's emotional curve, then deliberately make the *visual*-intensity curve track it: open space, increase tonal/color contrast, accelerate and complicate motion, tighten cut rhythm as the argument climaxes — then release toward affinity at resolution. **A flat visual-intensity line is the visual signature of "PPT."** The unforgivable failure is the *accidental mismatch*: a flat, static, low-contrast treatment laid over the beat that's supposed to be the climax — the viewer's body says "this isn't important" while the words say it's the most important thing.

**Deep space vs. flat space — the lever slides never touch.** Slides are relentlessly flat (everything on one plane). Deep space (overlapping planes, size differences, perspective, atmospheric haze, foreground/mid/background separation) is high-contrast and dynamic. This is where HyperFrames wins outright: layer stills into FG/MG/BG, parallax them, and a flat "slide" becomes deep, intensity-controllable space (see `cinematic-direction.md` → Multi-layer depth).

**Dominant contrast — per-frame attention discipline.** Every frame has exactly one place the eye goes first (the strongest contrast — tonal, color, or motion), and it must be the place that carries the meaning. Static assets neglect this because the whole image competes; force one focal point per beat.

**Support vs. counterpoint.** Visual curve *parallel* to the story curve = support (most common, reliable). Visuals *contradicting* the story = counterpoint (serene visuals over horror) — powerful, but must be a deliberate choice, never an accident.

## Layer 3 — THE FRAME (Bang · Mateu-Mestre · Katz · Mascelli)

*What goes inside a single frame, and how you stage the eye.* Emotion is **built from structure, not found in the subject** — you can engineer feeling with simple shapes and kill it with a beautiful-but-dead composition.

**Shape/size/placement/color as emotion dials (Bang):**
- **Shape:** pointed/jagged = threat; rounded/circular = safe. Horizontal = stable/calm; vertical = active/reaching; **diagonal = dynamic/tense** (the strongest static cue for motion).
- **Vertical position:** high in frame = freedom/triumph/aspiration (and precarious); low = heavy/grounded/oppressed (and safe). Animate a subject's vertical position to *move it along an emotional axis*.
- **Size = power.** Scale the dominant idea large, the vulnerable/minor element small. Don't default everything to comfortable mid-size (a PPT tell).
- **Center vs. edge:** dead-center = maximal stability/finality (reserve it); off-center = tension and exploration. Objects breaking the frame edge imply a world beyond it.
- **Color is the strongest thread.** We group by color more than by shape — a single charged accent (e.g. red for the stakes / the key term) binds meaning across scenes better than any repeated shape. Color also carries specific charge (red = danger/alarm/life; warm vs. cool temperature).
- **Avoid dead symmetry.** Perfectly balanced frames read as lifeless (Disney's "twins"); emotional truth lives between total order and total chaos.

**Value-first composition / notan (Mateu-Mestre).** Before detail, design each scene as light-and-dark shapes: where's the light, where's the dark, what single shape wins the eye? Dim the periphery and let one lit element carry the beat — this *is* the substrate doctrine, from the composition side. Match **complexity to emotion** (chaotic beat → busy frame; clarity beat → clean frame — don't run uniform complexity). Give each frame a **line of action** — a directional spine, often diagonal — so even a still has kinetic charge the camera move can ride.

**Shot vocabulary — deploy deliberately, never default (Katz + Mascelli):**
- **Shot size is an emphasis decision.** Sequence establishing → medium → close to control where interest sits. The **close-up is your emphasis weapon** — push in / scale up to the single detail that carries the beat; the wide *establishes* context. Don't leave everything at the same comfortable mid-size.
- **Angle is never neutral.** Low angle empowers, high angle diminishes, eye-level is neutral. Choose the "virtual camera" height for an emotional reason.
- **The contrasting-shots rule (a concrete cut test):** when cutting between two views of the *same* subject, change **both size and angle** decisively — or it reads as a jump-cut error.
- **Open vs. closed framing** as a mood control: box the frame for contained/tense beats, open it (content bleeding past edges) for expansive/free beats.
- **Stage in depth (FG/MG/BG)** — the direct technical cure for flatness, and what enables parallax.
- **Composition hygiene:** one center of interest; off-center balance (avoid dead-center, mergers, tangents — no background line growing out of a subject's head); **lead room** in the direction of motion or gaze.
- **A / I / L staging patterns:** when arranging multiple elements (two concepts in dialogue, three forces in tension), reach for a repeatable geometric template — apex/triangular (A), in-line (I), right-angle (L) — rather than improvising placement.

**Camera moves have meaning (choose to the beat, not for motion's sake):** push-in = commit/intensify; pull-out = reveal/contextualize/abandon; track = accompany; crane = grant overview/release; pan = redirect attention. Map each to the beat's intent.

## Layer 4 — MOTION (Williams + Disney's 12 principles)

The difference between "the card slides in" and "the card *arrives with weight*." (The seek-safe GSAP mechanics for all of this live in [`motion-principles.md`](./motion-principles.md); this is the *taste* that says when and why.)

**Timing vs. spacing — the deepest lever.** *Timing* = how long an action takes (the frames it occupies). *Spacing* = how positions distribute across those frames (clustered = slow, spread = fast). Same one-second timing, but with positions bunched at the ends and spread in the middle, transforms a mechanical glide into something alive. **Linear easing is "even spacing" — the #1 cause of PPT motion.** Kill it: use eased/spring curves (slow-in/slow-out) and deliberately *uneven* spacing for snap.

**The principles that matter most for motion-graphics:**
- **Anticipation + follow-through on every entrance/exit.** Don't just fade a card in — have it dip back slightly (anticipation), arrive with a small overshoot, settle (follow-through). Trailing elements lag the leader (overlap/drag). This is "placed" vs. "alive."
- **Arcs over straight lines.** Living motion travels curved paths; straight-line interpolation reads as robotic.
- **Slow in / slow out** — but don't over-ease everything into mush; sometimes a sharp, snapped move is right.
- **Weight via timing.** Heavy/important things accelerate slowly and take more frames; light things snap. Everything moving at one uniform speed is a core PPT tell.
- **The moving hold — critical for stunning stills.** A held image is *never* perfectly frozen, or it dies on screen. A slow push, parallax drift, or micro-scale keeps it breathing.
- **Exaggeration & staging.** Push the essential action past the literal to make it read; stage one clear idea per beat.
- **Structure-then-loosen workflow.** Block the key poses/beats first (pose-to-pose), then add secondary motion, overlap, and easing.

## Layer 5 — THE CUT (Murch)

Every scene boundary, every shot swap is a cut. Slides treat cuts as mechanical advances on the paragraph grid; the craft makes each cut feel inevitable.

**Cut on the idea, not the paragraph ("the blink").** We blink to punctuate thought — at the instant an idea completes and we turn to the next. A cut works when it lands where the viewer is *already ready to blink*: the moment they've finished absorbing one idea. Use word-level narration timing to cut at idea-completion, not at fixed durations. **This is the single biggest upgrade from slide-pacing.**

**The Rule of Six (rank the criteria; sacrifice from the bottom up):**

| Rank | Criterion | Weight | For an explainer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Emotion** | 51% | Does the cut serve the feeling this beat should carry? |
| 2 | **Story** | 23% | Does it advance the argument? |
| 3 | **Rhythm** | 10% | Is it at a rhythmically right point? |
| 4 | **Eye-trace** | 7% | Does it respect where the eye is / send it where it should go? |
| 5 | **Planarity (2D screen direction)** | 5% | Screen-direction / 180° consistency |
| 6 | **Spatial continuity (3D)** | 4% | Are things where they were in space? |

**Emotion (51%) outweighs the other five combined.** A cut that only satisfies rhythm (it's on the musical beat) but breaks the emotional/argumentative flow is *wrong*. Permission follows: if the emotionally right image breaks spatial logic, take it.

**Eye-trace across cuts.** Guide the eye to a zone *before* the cut, place the incoming focal point in a compatible zone *after* — the cut becomes seamless (or deliberately move the eye, then cut while it's in transit). With composed assets you control focal position exactly; exploit it so the eye never re-hunts.

**Match seam-hardness to the mental-jump size.** Same-topic beat → hard cut. Big conceptual/temporal leap → dissolve/fade to flag it. Don't cross-dissolve everything (mush), don't hard-cut across a major shift (jarring).

**The flinch test (lock a timing by feel).** Run a beat and hit a mark at the instant that feels right; run it again and check whether you flinch on the *same frame*. Same frame twice = something organically true about that moment. If your sense of "cut here" wanders each pass, the beat isn't resolved yet.

## McCloud's six panel-to-panel transitions (the sequencing texture)

*How* one shot relates to the next — the kind of gap the viewer must close. The diagnostic: **flat sequencing is everything action-to-action** — each frame advances the argument by exactly one beat, like a checklist. Vary the texture:

1. **Moment-to-moment** — one action in adjacent beats; minimal closure. *Slows time, adds weight.*
2. **Action-to-action** — one subject to its next distinct action. The workhorse; efficient. *(Overused → the checklist feel.)*
3. **Subject-to-subject** — same scene/idea, shift what we look at. More viewer involvement.
4. **Scene-to-scene** — a jump across time/space; the big deductive leap.
5. **Aspect-to-aspect** — no time passes; wander the details/mood of a place. *Builds atmosphere, doesn't advance.*
6. **Non-sequitur** — no logical relationship; pure juxtaposition.

Cure for flatness: deliberately inject **moment-to-moment** (let a beat breathe) and **aspect-to-aspect** (mood without advancing) among the action-to-action. This is the sequencing side of density rhythm.

## Sound as a second meaning channel (Murch / The Conversations)

Audio is *not a bed under the narration* — it's a parallel meaning channel that can carry emotion and theme **metaphorically**, not just literally (the *Apocalypse Now* helicopter/ceiling-fan blur). Score, ambience, and sound-motifs should do work the picture and narration can't. Build a small library of *specific* textures — specificity carries feeling. (Add this as a third track to the complementarity test: mute the visual, mute the narration, mute the sound — each should lose meaning.) Implementation lives in `hyperframes-media` (TTS, BGM, SFX) and `/media-use`.

## The complete per-scene workflow (all layers)

For an anchor scene, in order:

1. **State the controlling idea** and this scene's job: change, inspection, orientation, evidence, comparison or synthesis. Use the **value-turn** and **want** lenses where they apply. *(Layer 1)*
2. **Confirm a gap opens** — what expectation is set, then violated? *(Layer 1)*
3. **Thumbnail the notan and emotion** — light/dark shapes, one focal point; set shape/size/placement/color to the beat's feeling. *(Layers 2–3)*
4. **Choose shot size / angle / depth / camera move** for meaning; lock screen direction; stage in FG/MG/BG. *(Layer 3)*
5. **Place it on the act's intensity progression** — is this a build, a peak, or a release? Set contrast accordingly. *(Layer 2)*
6. **Animate with intent** — spacing, anticipation/arcs/overlap, or an intentional still hold where reading and narration need it. *(Layer 4)*
7. **Cut on the idea** using the flinch test; match seam-hardness to the jump; vary the transition type (not all action-to-action). *(Layers 4–5, McCloud)*
8. **Add sound** as a parallel channel where it earns a slot. *(Conversations)*

This is a superset checklist — most scenes touch only a few steps. The awe layer's Effort Protocol decides *how hard* to push a shot; this decides *what craft levers* to reach for.
